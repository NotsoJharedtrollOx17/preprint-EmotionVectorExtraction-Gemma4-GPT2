\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) often generate language that appears emotionally
appropriate: they can respond empathetically to distress, adopt an angry tone
when describing harm, or express enthusiasm about positive outcomes.  Such
behavior alone is not evidence that a model has subjective experience.  A more
tractable scientific question is whether a model contains internal
representations of \emph{emotion concepts} that are activated in relevant
contexts and that causally affect its output.  This question sits naturally
within mechanistic interpretability, which seeks to connect internal
activations and computations to model behavior rather than relying only on
input--output correlations \cite{geva_transformer_2021,sawant_mechanistic_2026}.

Sofroniew et al.\ \cite{sofroniew_emotion_2026} introduced the term
\emph{emotion vectors} for directions in Claude Sonnet 4.5's residual stream
that represent broad emotion concepts.  They reported that these directions
generalize beyond explicit emotion words, organize partly along the
psychological dimensions of valence and arousal, and influence generated
behavior under activation steering.  Their results motivate an important
generality question: are comparable affective directions a property of one
frontier, proprietary model, or can they also be recovered in independently
available Transformer language models?

This work provides a partial, resource-constrained replication of three parts
of that investigation in \texttt{openai-community/gpt-2-medium} and
\texttt{google/gemma-4-e2b}.  We construct emotion prototypes from generated
stories and a neutral reference corpus, extract normalized contrast vectors at
an intermediate residual-stream layer, and assess them with (i) Logit Lens
vocabulary projections, (ii) PCA and pairwise cosine-similarity analyses, and
(iii) activation steering on two neutral prompts.  We evaluate both a
nine-emotion set and a 20-emotion extension, allowing us to examine whether
the qualitative findings persist as nearby affective categories are added.

Our contribution is deliberately narrower than a full replication of
Sofroniew et al.  We do not test the original study's behavioral or alignment
evaluations, search across layers, or estimate correlations with external
human valence--arousal ratings.  Instead, we ask whether this lightweight
pipeline recovers interpretable, structured, and behaviorally consequential
directions in two models with substantially different age, scale, and training
regimes.  In our experiments, both models yield emotion vectors with a
valence-like separation and semantically meaningful similarity structure;
steering visibly changes token probabilities and continuations, with stronger
effects in GPT-2 Medium.  These observations are evidence for portability of
the procedure, not evidence that either model experiences emotion.
